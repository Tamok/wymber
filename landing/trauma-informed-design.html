Trauma-informed design, applied

By JELL, Wymber · Last updated 2026-06-12

Trauma-informed design means building software on the assumption that some of your users carry trauma, and making sure the product never re-creates the dynamics that hurt them: surprise, pressure, loss of control, being trapped. The reference framework is SAMHSA's six guiding principles: safety; trust and transparency; peer support; collaboration; empowerment, voice and choice; and cultural humility. Principles are easy to cite and hard to ship, so here is what they actually become in Wymber, decision by decision.

Safety: nothing sudden, nothing loud

  • Soft pastels, generous spacing, and slow (0.3s) transitions; no jarring motion, and prefers-reduced-motion is honoured throughout.
  • Esc always closes what's open. You're never trapped in a screen, and with nothing open it locks Wymber instantly: a quick exit that's always available.
  • Crisis support (call or text 988, 911) is one tap away on every screen, without ever being pushed at you.

Trust and transparency: claims you can check

  • The privacy promise is architecture, not policy: encrypted on your device, no account, nothing transmitted, all of it auditable in the open code.
  • Honest limits are stated where they matter (what encryption protects you from and what it can't), because reassurance that overpromises is its own harm.

Empowerment, voice and choice: you set the pace

  • Nothing is mandatory and nothing nags. There are no streaks, reminders, or guilt mechanics; trauma-informed content design calls these out as quiet coercion, and they have no place here.
  • Prompts are optional and non-directive ("What happened, in your words?" never "Describe your trauma"). A discovery suggestion is a question, never a change to your map.
  • Onboarding is brief, skippable, and reopenable, following empathy-centred UX guidance for mental-health apps; stepping away is treated as normal, not as churn. The window of tolerance (Dan Siegel's term) shapes this: the tool should help you stay regulated, not push you past it.

Accessibility as part of safety

Predictability is an accessibility property too. Wymber is built to WCAG 2.1 AA: fully keyboard-navigable, screen-reader friendly (the canvas has a synchronized list twin, so the map is never visual-only), with three themes including a low-contrast soft mode. For someone overwhelmed, a UI that behaves exactly as expected is not a nicety; it's what makes the tool usable at all.
