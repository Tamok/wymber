How do you do the trauma egg exercise?

By JELL, Wymber · Written to trauma-informed principles · Last updated 2026-07-06

You do the trauma egg exercise by drawing a large egg shape on paper and filling it, from the bottom up, with the painful experiences of your life in the order they happened. The earliest memory of feeling powerless goes at the base. Above it you stack a small oval for each significant event, roughly by age, working toward the present. In the corners you note the people and rules that shaped you, and at the top you name the recurring themes and the beliefs those events left behind. The result is a single vertical picture of your emotional history, seen at a glance.

The Trauma Egg was created by Marilyn Murray in the early 1980s as a foundational tool of her wider approach. She has developed and refined the Murray Method since 1983. What follows is an educational, paper-first walkthrough with a short example, so if you have searched for a "trauma egg example" you can see one concretely.

Before you start: go gently

Revisiting painful memories can bring up strong feelings, sometimes without warning. There is no prize for doing this quickly or all at once. Give yourself permission to pause, to write only a word instead of a whole story, or to close the notebook and come back another day. Many people find this exercise most useful when they do it alongside a therapist who can help hold what surfaces.

If at any point you feel unsafe or in crisis, please reach out for support. In the US, you can call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), or call 911 in an emergency. This article is not a crisis service and not a substitute for professional care.

The step-by-step exercise (on paper)

You need only a large sheet of paper and a pen. Turn the paper so it is tall rather than wide, because your history will grow upward.

  1. Draw a large egg. Fill most of the page with one big oval, narrower at the top and wider at the bottom. This outline holds your whole story.
  2. Place your earliest memory at the base. Near the bottom, note the first time you remember feeling small, frightened, or powerless. It does not have to be dramatic or "the worst thing." It only has to be the earliest one you can find.
  3. Mark the corners. In or near the four corners, write brief notes on the people and structures that shaped your early world: your father, your mother, the unspoken family rules ("we don't talk about feelings"), and the roles you were handed (the peacemaker, the strong one, the invisible one).
  4. Stack an oval for each event, in order. Moving upward from the base, add a small oval for each significant painful experience, placed roughly by the age it happened. Bottom is early childhood; the top approaches today. A few words inside each oval is plenty.
  5. Name the recurring themes. Step back and look at the whole egg. Do certain feelings repeat, abandonment, shame, not being believed, having to be perfect? Write these themes along the sides or top.
  6. List the beliefs that formed. At the very top, note the conclusions you drew from all of this ("I have to earn love," "It isn't safe to need anyone"). These are often the beliefs still steering how you react today, long after you stopped noticing them.
  7. Optional: write a gentler line for the future. Some people close by writing one kinder sentence they would like to grow toward. This is optional, and there is no right answer.

A short worked example

Here is a brief, anonymized egg so the shape is concrete:

  • Base: "Age 4, hid in the closet during a loud argument, no one came."
  • Corners: Father, often absent. Mother, anxious and overwhelmed. Rule: "keep the peace." Role: the quiet, easy child.
  • Stacked ovals: Age 7, moved schools and felt invisible. Age 11, a teacher humiliated me in front of the class. Age 15, a friend betrayed a secret. Age 22, a breakup that felt like proof.
  • Themes: feeling unseen; bracing for rejection; keeping everyone else calm.
  • Beliefs: "If I stay small and useful, I won't be left."

Laid out this way, a person might notice that a reaction in adulthood that felt "silly" seems to echo something four ovals down. Many people find that connections which are hard to feel become easier to see once they are on the page, though what you take from the picture is entirely your own.

What to do with it afterward

Once the egg exists, it becomes something to reflect on rather than to complete. You might sit with a single oval and ask, "How does this still show up now?" You might bring the whole page to a therapist, who can help you make sense of the patterns safely and at a pace that suits you. There is no requirement to share it with anyone. It is yours.
