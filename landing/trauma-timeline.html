How do you make a trauma timeline?

By JELL, Wymber · Written to trauma-informed principles · Last updated 2026-07-10

You make a trauma timeline by drawing one long horizontal line on paper, anchoring your earliest years at one end and today at the other, and then placing significant experiences along it in the order they happened. Each event gets a short label and a rough age, nothing more. When you step back, you see your history as a single line you can take in at a glance, instead of a story you have to hold in your head all at once.

Timeline exercises turn up all over trauma and recovery work: therapists use them to get an overview of a person's history, recovery programs use them in written step work, and narrative approaches use similar "lifeline" exercises to put a life story outside the person, where it is easier to look at. Unlike the trauma egg, which is Marilyn Murray's specific trademarked exercise, the timeline is a common format with no single owner. What follows is an educational, paper-first walkthrough in that shared tradition, with reflection prompts for afterward.

An honest note on what this is. This page is an independent, educational how-to from the maker of Wymber, a privacy-first mapping tool. It is not therapy, not a clinical protocol, and not medical advice. A timeline is a reflection exercise; whether and when to do one is entirely your choice, and it can wait for a steadier day.

Before you start: go gently

Putting hard events in a row can stir up more than you expect. The order itself is sometimes the tender part: seeing one thing follow another can make old connections suddenly plain. So decide before you begin that you are allowed to do this partially, slowly, and imperfectly. A single word can stand in for a whole year.

Therapists describe a window of tolerance, the zone where you can touch difficult material and still stay present. If you notice yourself going numb, or your heart racing, or the room feeling far away, treat that as a signal to pause: stand up, feel your feet on the floor, name five things you can see. The timeline will keep. Many people also find this exercise most useful alongside a therapist who can help hold what surfaces.

If at any point you feel unsafe or in crisis, please reach out for support. In the US, you can call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), or call 911 in an emergency. This article is not a crisis service and not a substitute for professional care.

The step-by-step exercise (on paper)

You need a large sheet of paper and a pen. Turn the paper sideways, wide rather than tall, because this history reads left to right. If one sheet feels cramped, tape two together.

  1. Draw one long line. A single horizontal line across the middle of the page, with a little empty margin beyond both ends.
  2. Anchor the ends. Mark the left end with your birth or earliest years and the right end with today. Leave the space past "today" open on purpose: the timeline stops at the present, your life does not.
  3. Add events as they come to mind, not in order. Memory is not chronological, and you do not have to be either. Let experiences surface in whatever order they arrive, then place each one where it belongs on the line. A few words and a rough age is plenty ("age 9, grandmother died").
  4. Put hard and steadying things both on the page. A common variant puts painful events below the line and steadying ones above it: the teacher who noticed, a friendship, a good year. This is not about forcing gratitude; it keeps the picture honest, because both kinds of things are your history.
  5. Mark the eras, if they help. Some chapters are not events but stretches ("the loud years at home", "the gray stretch after the move"). A labeled bracket under a span of the line holds these without asking you to itemize them.
  6. Stop while you still have something left. A timeline built across several sittings is just as real as one made in an afternoon, and usually kinder. Put the page somewhere private and come back when you choose to.
  7. Step back and look. When enough is down, read the whole line from left to right at arm's length. You are not looking for anything in particular yet; you are just seeing your history in sequence, maybe for the first time.

Reflection prompts

These are questions, not homework. Take one that draws you and leave the rest.

  • Where do events cluster? What was happening around you in those years?
  • Where are the quiet stretches? What, or who, carried you through them?
  • Which mark still feels close, as if it were far more recent than it is?
  • Are there places where the line seems to change direction, even slightly? What changed?
  • Is anything easier to see laid out in a line than it was to say in words?
  • If you added one steadying thing you had forgotten, what would it be?

A short worked example

Here is a brief, anonymized timeline so the shape is concrete:

  • Left anchor: born.
  • Age 6: moved towns, lost first friends (below the line).
  • Age 9: grandmother died (below); the teacher who noticed (above).
  • Ages 12 to 15: a bracket labeled "the loud years at home".
  • Age 17: left home early (below); first job, own money (above).
  • Age 20: met R., things got steadier (above).
  • Right anchor: today.

Laid out this way, a person might notice the empty gap between 9 and 12 and wonder what filled it, or see that the steadier stretch began earlier than they had thought. What you notice on yours is your own.

Timeline, trauma egg, or a map?

They are siblings. A trauma timeline holds sequence: what happened, in what order, and how close together. The trauma egg stacks the same history by theme, with family rules in the corners and beliefs at the top. And trauma mapping holds connections: which events, feelings, triggers, and supports link to which. If order feels like the missing piece, start with a timeline. If themes and beliefs do, try the egg. If it is the web between things you want to see, mapping is built for that. There is no wrong door, and nothing stops you from doing one on paper this month and another next year.

What to do with it afterward

Once the timeline exists, it becomes something to sit with rather than to complete. You might return to one mark and ask how it still shows up now. You might bring the page to a therapist, who can help you make sense of what it holds safely and at your own pace. There is no requirement to share it with anyone. It is yours.
